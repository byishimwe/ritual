# **1\. Concept Overview**

## **1.1 The Central Idea**

Ritual is a cinematic interactive experience centered on the familiar process of making espresso.

The visitor encounters a believable automatic bean-to-cup espresso machine and discovers that it can be operated directly.

Rather than clicking through a traditional website or watching a predetermined product animation, the visitor interacts with physical components of the machine.

They open the bean hopper, add coffee beans, position a cup, power on the machine, initiate its preparation sequence, observe the mechanical process, and control the dispensing of the finished coffee.

The machine responds to their actions through movement, sound, changes in physical state, light, and visible consequences.

The result should feel like an interactive product film.

The screen should not communicate that the visitor is navigating software.

It should communicate that the visitor is operating an object.

## **1.2 What the Project Is Really About**

The coffee machine is the visible subject, but the deeper creative subject is a process.

That process consists of:

**Discovery → Preparation → Activation → Transformation → Anticipation → Control → Result**

The experience takes a familiar activity and deliberately reveals the gestures, mechanical operations, pauses, and consequences that make it possible.

The visitor supplies the ingredients and performs the meaningful actions.

The machine performs the internal transformation.

The camera, sound, and lighting elevate that transformation into a cinematic experience.

## **1.3 Creative Thesis**

> An ordinary real-world process can become an extraordinary digital experience when its physical interactions, movement, timing, and presentation are carefully choreographed.

Ritual should demonstrate that cinematic web experiences do not require fictional environments, futuristic interfaces, or overwhelming visual complexity.

A recognizable machine can become compelling through its physicality, responsiveness, atmosphere, and relationship with the visitor.

## **1.4 The Intended Feeling**

The experience should feel:

* Physical rather than abstract.  
* Cinematic rather than theatrical.  
* Premium rather than extravagant.  
* Minimal rather than empty.  
* Interactive rather than automated.  
* Believable rather than mechanically exhaustive.  
* Calm but never lifeless.  
* Engaging without becoming game-like.

The artistic identity should emerge from the quality of the complete experience rather than from individual decorative effects.

# **2\. Core Creative Philosophy**

## **2.1 Familiarity Over Fiction**

Ritual must remain grounded in recognizable physical reality.

The machine should look like something that could plausibly exist as a premium consumer appliance.

The experience explicitly rejects:

* Science-fiction product design.  
* Fictional technology.  
* Unrecognizable abstract machinery.  
* Unnecessary fantasy.  
* Futuristic holographic interfaces.  
* Excessive visual ornament.  
* Stylization that compromises product believability.

The creative approach is not to invent an extraordinary object.

It is to make an ordinary object feel extraordinary through its presentation and behavior.

## **2.2 Reality, Art-Directed**

The machine does not need to be a mechanically exact reproduction of a commercial product.

The design may simplify or reinterpret certain physical details to create a more understandable and engaging experience.

Art direction may exaggerate:

* Mechanical movement.  
* Animation timing.  
* Material highlights.  
* Camera movement.  
* Acoustic detail.  
* Visible feedback.  
* Environmental atmosphere.  
* The perception of internal processing.

However, these decisions must reinforce the idea that the machine has weight, materials, purpose, and recognizable mechanisms.

The guiding relationship is:

**Physical plausibility \+ deliberate artistic exaggeration**

Not every underlying mechanism needs to be simulated.

What matters is whether the resulting action appears coherent and convincing.

## **2.3 The Machine Is the Interface**

The experience contains no conventional visible interface.

The visitor interacts with physical components directly.

Shape, position, scale, mechanical affordances, lighting, sound, and animation communicate the possibilities.

The machine is both the subject and the primary interaction system.

## **2.4 Physical Consequence Over Decorative Response**

Every important action must produce a consequence related to the object.

Opening a lid should reveal the hopper.

Adding beans should visibly supply the machine.

Pressing Power should wake the machine.

Pressing Make should begin preparation.

Pressing Pour should release coffee.

Moving the cup should affect where that coffee lands.

The visitor should develop an intuitive understanding that the digital object obeys consistent rules.

## **2.5 Polish Over Feature Count**

The experience must remain focused.

A small number of carefully implemented interactions will produce a stronger result than a larger collection of incomplete, mechanically disconnected, or visually inconsistent features.

Refinement should prioritize:

* Motion quality.  
* Physical believability.  
* Interaction responsiveness.  
* Visual hierarchy.  
* Pacing.  
* Sound synchronization.  
* Camera direction.  
* Reliable behavior.

# **3\. Reference & Research Direction**

## **3.1 Reference Strategy**

The reference process should study two complementary subjects:

**Physical product design**

How real automatic espresso machines are shaped, manufactured, assembled, operated, and visually organized.

**Product photography and cinematography**

How real products are presented through lighting, composition, reflections, camera placement, atmosphere, and visual rhythm.

The goal is not to copy a single commercial coffee machine.

It is to synthesize recognizable design characteristics into an original but believable machine.

## **3.2 Product Design References**

The primary research category is compact automatic bean-to-cup espresso machines.

Relevant references should help establish:

| Reference area | Questions to answer |
| ----- | ----- |
| Overall silhouette | What makes the object immediately recognizable as a coffee machine? |
| Chassis | How do large physical masses relate to one another? |
| Hopper | How are beans stored and accessed? |
| Lid | Where is the hinge and how does it move? |
| Controls | How are important operations represented physically? |
| Outlet | Where does coffee emerge, and how is its destination visually communicated? |
| Cup platform | How is the receiving area organized? |
| Materials | Which surfaces are metal, plastic, transparent, or ceramic? |
| Manufacturing | Where are seams, joints, bevels, and transitions useful? |
| Scale | How do the machine, beans, and cup relate proportionally? |

Real products should provide the foundation for form, scale, and functional credibility.

## **3.3 Reference Selection Principles**

A suitable reference should contribute at least one of the following:

* A recognizable silhouette.  
* A convincing functional component.  
* A useful material treatment.  
* A clear physical interaction.  
* An effective composition.  
* A believable mechanical detail.

References should not be added merely because they look visually expensive or complex.

The selected machine should remain feasible to model and operate in a browser.

## **3.4 Product Cinematography References**

Study restrained premium product photography for:

* Large soft highlights.  
* Controlled reflections.  
* Dark-material readability.  
* Silhouette separation.  
* Foreground and background depth.  
* Close-up composition.  
* Material contrast.  
* Deliberate camera movement.  
* Visual emphasis on functional components.

The objective is to make the machine feel physically present within the frame.

It should never appear as an isolated, unlit 3D asset placed against an arbitrary background.

## **3.5 Interaction Reference**

The original interactive presentation reference is:

[https\://www\.jesse-zhou.com/](https://www.jesse-zhou.com/)

The relevant principle is the ability to inspect a centered three-dimensional setup while retaining an intentionally composed visual presentation.

Ritual should adopt the principle of controlled spatial exploration, not copy the referenced site's visual identity, exact camera behavior, or artistic content.

## **3.6 Reference Research Boundaries**

References must not introduce new requirements simply because a commercial machine happens to possess a particular feature.

For example, observing additional knobs, milk systems, displays, trays, or drink controls does not mean they should be added to Ritual.

The project has a fixed central process.

References exist to make that process more believable and visually refined.

# **4\. Exact Machine Concept**

## **4.1 Machine Type**

**Automatic Bean-to-Cup Espresso Coffee Machine**

This machine category is locked.

Its conceptual operation is:

1. Receive whole coffee beans.  
2. Grind the beans.  
3. Brew the coffee internally.  
4. Hold the prepared output until the dispensing action.  
5. Release coffee through a dedicated outlet.  
6. Deliver coffee into the receiving cup when correctly positioned.

The separate preparation and dispensing stages are intentional experience-design choices.

They create anticipation and preserve direct user control of the final visual payoff.

The machine should remain visually believable even where its interaction sequence is artistically simplified.

## **4.2 Form Factor**

The machine should resemble a compact premium consumer appliance rather than a large commercial café machine.

The original reference range is:

| Dimension | Approximate range |
| ----- | ----- |
| Width | 30–40 cm |
| Depth | 35–45 cm |
| Height | 35–45 cm |

A working target of approximately **35 cm wide × 40 cm deep × 40 cm high** is consistent with the concept.

These values are design references, not verified final model measurements.

The production dimensions and coordinate system will be established in Document 02\.

## **4.3 Proportion Objectives**

The machine should have sufficient vertical presence for the hopper and enough frontal area to communicate the controls, outlet, and receiving cup.

Its depth should produce interesting three-quarter compositions without making the object feel unnecessarily bulky.

The overall silhouette must remain recognizable at the intended exhibition viewing distance.

## **4.4 Physical Hierarchy**

The design follows one main relationship:

**Coffee beans → Hopper → Machine → Outlet → Cup**

This relationship provides:

* A physical organization.  
* An interaction hierarchy.  
* A compositional hierarchy.  
* A storytelling structure.

The visitor should understand the machine's input and output through the spatial arrangement alone.

## **4.5 Form Language**

The design should balance simple large masses with restrained functional detail.

The body provides the dominant silhouette.

The hopper creates a distinct upper feature.

The front controls and coffee outlet establish the functional face of the machine.

The cup creates a visual destination beneath the outlet.

Bevels, seams, material boundaries, and mechanical details should exist where they improve form or realism.

The design should avoid unnecessary complexity that becomes distracting in the web presentation.

# **5\. Physical Component Specification**

## **5.1 Component Philosophy**

Every visible component should contribute meaningfully to at least one of the following:

* Interaction.  
* Physical operation.  
* Recognizability.  
* Visual composition.  
* Realism.  
* Storytelling.

A component that contributes to none of these objectives should generally be excluded.

## **5.2 Required Component Inventory**

| Component | Function | Conceptual priority |
| ----- | ----- | ----- |
| Main body | Defines silhouette and product identity | Essential |
| Bean hopper | Contains supplied beans | Essential |
| Hopper lid | Allows physical access to hopper | Essential |
| Grinding/processing region | Communicates internal preparation | Essential |
| Coffee outlet | Dispensing origin | Essential |
| Cup platform/drip area | Establishes output destination | Essential |
| Cup | Receives liquid and supports placement interaction | Essential |
| Coffee beans/container | Provides physical ingredient input | Essential |
| Power button | Activates the machine | Essential |
| Make button | Begins preparation | Essential |
| Pour button | Controls dispensing | Essential |
| Coffee liquid | Communicates the final output | Essential |
| Integrated indicator | Provides subtle machine feedback | Optional/secondary |
| Steam | Reinforces heat and completion | Secondary |
| Surface spill | Represents incorrect or prolonged pouring | Secondary |

## **5.3 Main Body**

The body forms the dominant visual mass.

It should communicate structural solidity and product quality.

Its surfaces should support attractive reflections and highlights without requiring excessive geometry.

The body should feel like an assembled manufactured product, not a collection of unrelated primitives.

## **5.4 Bean Hopper**

The hopper should be visually recognizable as the ingredient-receiving component.

It should be positioned where a visitor can naturally associate it with the beans.

Its materials should reveal or suggest the stored beans.

The hopper must remain visible enough during exploration for the visitor to discover its function.

## **5.5 Hopper Lid**

The lid is a primary interactive component.

Its construction should visibly suggest that it can open.

The motion must imply a real hinge and a plausible mechanical range.

Its opening and closing should communicate weight and resistance through controlled animation.

The exact pivot, rotation axis, angular range, and collision constraints belong in the asset and interaction specifications.

## **5.6 Coffee Beans & Container**

Beans are an important part of the experience because they represent physical input.

The visitor should be able to pick up or manipulate the beans or their container and transfer them into the hopper.

The beans should not simply disappear when clicked.

The action should visibly communicate transfer:

**Pick up → Move to hopper → Release → Fall → Settle**

A carefully directed animation may replace complex physics, provided the visitor still experiences a meaningful manipulation.

The beans should be recognizable as roasted coffee beans rather than generic brown particles.

## **5.7 Grinding & Processing Region**

A complete physical reconstruction of the internal grinder is unnecessary.

The machine may communicate grinding through selected internal movement, sound, bean consumption, vibration, and machine feedback.

The visitor must perceive mechanical work occurring.

The emphasis is credible behavior rather than technical cross-sectional accuracy.

## **5.8 Coffee Outlet**

The outlet is one of the most important visual anchors.

It provides the origin of the final liquid effect.

Its relationship with the cup should be immediately understandable.

The outlet and cup must form a convincing physical vertical alignment during pouring.

## **5.9 Cup Platform**

The platform provides a stable visual relationship between machine, outlet, and cup.

It should appear physically suitable for holding a cup and receiving occasional liquid.

It should also support believable contact shadows and scale.

The platform does not need to be removable or separately interactive.

## **5.10 Cup**

The cup is essential to the experience.

Its role is to receive the dispensed coffee.

It should be physically movable and placed independently by the visitor.

The cup should have an understandable resting location before placement, allowing positioning to become an intentional action.

The exact starting coordinates and permitted movement area will be finalized in Document 02 and Document 03\.

Its ceramic material should provide contrast with the darker machine and allow the filling liquid to remain visible.

## **5.11 Physical Buttons**

There are exactly three primary functional controls:

1. Power.  
2. Make Coffee.  
3. Pour.

They should look like physical machine controls.

Their scale, placement, surface treatment, and travel distance should imply that they can be pressed.

Their behavior must remain consistent with their individual responsibilities.

## **5.12 Coffee Liquid**

The coffee must read as warm, dark, slightly viscous liquid.

It should have appropriate specular response, movement, and contact behavior.

The liquid is central to the project's final visual payoff.

Its presentation deserves more refinement than an ordinary secondary effect.

## **5.13 Components Excluded from the Concept**

Do not automatically introduce:

* Decorative dials.  
* Additional buttons.  
* Unnecessary knobs.  
* Steam wands.  
* Complex digital displays.  
* Removable drip trays.  
* Decorative machinery.  
* Excessive screws.  
* Unnecessary seams.  
* Fictional mechanisms.  
* Complicated coffee-configuration controls.

These elements may exist on real commercial products, but are unnecessary to Ritual's selected process.

# **6\. Visual Identity & Material Language**

## **6.1 Primary Aesthetic**

**Warm Minimalism \+ Premium Real Product Design**

The desired result should feel contemporary, carefully manufactured, restrained, and tactile.

The object should possess enough detail to feel believable without sacrificing a clean overall silhouette.

## **6.2 Material Hierarchy**

| Material | Intended character | Application |
| ----- | ----- | ----- |
| Charcoal semi-matte finish | Solid, premium, restrained | Main body and selected panels |
| Brushed satin metal | Precision, structure, soft reflection | Fascia and functional trim |
| Dark plastic/rubber | Functional and tactile | Controls, joints, selected details |
| Transparent or tinted hopper | Lightweight, functional, revealing | Bean container |
| Roasted coffee beans | Natural, organic, slightly varied | Ingredient |
| Off-white ceramic | Warm, refined, contrasting | Cup |
| Dark brown liquid | Warm, glossy, fluid | Prepared coffee |
| Subtle stone/wood/composite | Grounded and neutral | Supporting surface |

## **6.3 Machine Body**

The body should not rely on mirror-like chrome or excessively polished metallic materials.

A combination of charcoal, brushed metal, and subtle dark trim offers more controlled material contrast.

Large soft highlights should reveal curvature and bevels.

Roughness variation should create a believable manufactured surface.

## **6.4 Hopper Material**

The hopper should be transparent or lightly tinted.

Transparency must allow the ingredient to remain visually identifiable without making the hopper disappear.

Material thickness, reflections, and highlights should communicate a real physical container.

## **6.5 Coffee Beans**

Coffee beans should use natural dark-brown variations with subtle highlights.

Their surfaces should appear roasted rather than wet, plastic, or artificially glossy.

Individual bean detail may be simplified where small scale makes that detail imperceptible.

## **6.6 Coffee Liquid**

The coffee should be visibly distinct from the bean material.

It requires appropriate highlights and reflections that allow the viewer to read movement and surface curvature.

A dark material without controlled specular response risks appearing like a black rod or flat surface rather than liquid.

## **6.7 Cup**

Warm white or off-white ceramic is the preferred direction.

The cup provides visual contrast and creates a readable boundary for the rising coffee level.

Its geometry should remain clean and familiar.

## **6.8 Supporting Surface**

Possible surface treatments include restrained stone, natural wood, or neutral composite.

The supporting surface should have enough texture to feel real, but not so much that it competes with the machine.

## **6.9 Material Quality Standard**

Materials must feel related to one another.

Each surface should receive lighting consistently and communicate its physical character.

Avoid:

* Excessively glossy plastics.  
* Flat, lifeless metal.  
* Overly transparent glass.  
* Cartoon-like coffee.  
* Artificial material saturation.  
* Inconsistent surface scale.  
* Strong texture noise that distracts from the object.

# **7\. Scene & Environmental Direction**

## **7.1 Product-Stage Concept**

The environment should feel situated between a real countertop and a cinematic product-photography set.

The stage exists to make the machine believable without requiring an entire room.

It provides grounding, reflections, shadows, atmosphere, and spatial depth.

It is not a separate attraction.

## **7.2 Required Environment**

The scene should contain:

* The central coffee machine.  
* The coffee beans/container.  
* The ceramic cup.  
* A supporting countertop or platform.  
* A minimal background or backdrop.  
* Necessary contact shadows and environmental lighting.  
* Only supporting geometry that improves the experience.

## **7.3 Scene Hierarchy**

**Primary:** Coffee machine.

**Secondary:** Bean hopper and ingredient.

**Secondary:** Outlet and cup.

**Tertiary:** Countertop and backdrop.

Everything outside the main machine interaction should remain subordinate.

## **7.4 Centered Composition**

The scene should be centered around the machine.

The experience does not require an explorable kitchen or an expansive 3D world.

Even during camera movement, the composition should remain organized around the main product.

The viewer should always be able to return to an understandable machine-centered perspective.

## **7.5 Atmosphere**

Atmospheric effects should remain subtle.

Potential elements include:

* Soft environmental depth.  
* Gentle material highlights.  
* Natural shadow falloff.  
* Very restrained airborne particles.  
* Subtle steam during the coffee process.

Atmosphere must not resemble fantasy fog, magical particles, or theatrical volumetric spectacle.

## **7.6 Environmental Restraint**

Avoid:

* Large furnished interiors.  
* Decorative shelving.  
* Unnecessary kitchen appliances.  
* Plants or props added only to fill space.  
* Dramatic architecture competing with the machine.  
* Strong background patterns.  
* Distracting animated environmental elements.

The product and the process are sufficient to carry the experience.

# **8\. The No-UI Experience**

## **8.1 Non-Negotiable Rule**

The active experience must contain no conventional visible interface.

Do not introduce:

* Titles.  
* Navigation bars.  
* Explanatory paragraphs.  
* Tooltips.  
* External buttons.  
* Instruction prompts.  
* On-screen progress indicators.  
* Textual status messages.  
* Game-like overlays.  
* Tutorial sequences.  
* Artificial completion screens.

The visual field should primarily contain the physical scene itself.

## **8.2 Discoverability Through Physical Affordances**

Since the experience has no textual instructions, the physical design must communicate what is interactive.

**Hopper:** Its lid, position, and connection to the beans should invite exploration.

**Bean container:** Its proximity and relationship to the hopper should suggest a transfer action.

**Cup:** Its shape and placement should make its receiving role understandable.

**Buttons:** Their geometry should communicate pressability.

**Outlet:** Its alignment with the cup platform should communicate the destination of the prepared coffee.

## **8.3 Feedback Without Instruction**

Feedback should confirm actions instead of explaining them verbally.

Examples:

| Action | Appropriate feedback |
| ----- | ----- |
| Open hopper | Lid moves, subtle mechanical sound |
| Add beans | Beans fall and settle |
| Close hopper | Lid closes with tactile sound |
| Move cup | Cup follows the user's gesture and settles |
| Press Power | Physical depression and machine activation |
| Press Make | Button response and mechanical activity |
| Grinding begins | Motor sound, vibration, relevant animation |
| Brewing completes | Internal motion and sound stop; subtle readiness indication |
| Press Pour | Coffee begins flowing |
| Press Pour again | Coffee stops flowing |

## **8.4 Integrated Machine Feedback**

Machine-integrated lights or mechanical indicators may be used where they improve clarity.

These should feel like actual features of the appliance.

They should not resemble HTML progress bars, floating interface panels, or software notifications.

A real machine's minimal physical markings or symbols may be considered if necessary for recognizable control operation, but they must remain part of the object rather than becoming a separate interface.

## **8.5 Discovery vs Frustration**

The absence of instructions must not be used to justify obscure interaction design.

A visitor should be able to infer relationships through proximity, shape, feedback, and repeated experimentation.

If a primary interaction consistently goes undiscovered during testing, the first response should be to improve its physical affordance or presentation rather than immediately adding instructional text.

# **9\. Primary Interaction Design**

## **9.1 Interaction Categories**

Ritual contains four principal categories of visitor interaction:

1. Opening and closing the hopper.  
2. Adding coffee beans.  
3. Positioning and moving the cup.  
4. Operating the machine's physical controls.

Camera exploration provides the supporting spatial interaction.

## **9.2 Opening the Hopper**

The visitor interacts directly with the hopper lid.

The lid rotates around a plausible hinge and reveals the compartment beneath.

Movement should have appropriate mechanical character.

It should neither teleport open nor swing weightlessly.

The interaction should remain understandable from the initial or easily reachable camera positions.

## **9.3 Adding Beans**

The visitor manipulates the ingredient or its container and moves it toward the hopper.

The transfer should visibly represent coffee beans entering the compartment.

The interaction may use an authored movement sequence, a controlled drag, or another physically convincing approach.

The important requirement is that the visitor performs an intentional ingredient-transfer action.

The beans should not be supplied through an unrelated on-screen control.

## **9.4 Closing the Hopper**

Closing the lid completes the ingredient preparation.

The lid should settle into its correct physical position.

The mechanical response should communicate closure without an exaggerated animation.

## **9.5 Moving the Cup**

The cup can be repositioned beneath the outlet.

The visitor should feel that they are moving a physical ceramic object rather than adjusting an abstract numeric value.

The permitted movement should be sufficient to demonstrate alignment and misalignment.

It should remain constrained to a believable interaction area.

The exact geometry, movement axes, and alignment thresholds are to be specified in Document 03\.

## **9.6 Power Control**

Power is a distinct physical control.

Pressing it in the appropriate condition wakes the machine.

The primary experience is preparation-first: bean preparation must not require the machine to be powered on.

The precise rules for early power activation, power-off, and interruption during processing will be formalized later.

## **9.7 Make Control**

Make initiates the internal preparation process.

It must not instantly produce coffee.

It must not duplicate an active brewing sequence.

The visitor's press starts an autonomous mechanical performance comprising grinding, a pause, and brewing.

## **9.8 Pour Control**

Pour becomes meaningful when preparation is complete.

The first valid press begins dispensing.

A subsequent press stops dispensing.

The machine must visibly respond to this control rather than completing a fixed pour regardless of the visitor's action.

# **10\. The Preparation-First Experience Sequence**

## **10.1 Canonical Journey**

The canonical visitor journey is:

**Discover → Open Hopper → Add Beans → Close Hopper → Position Cup → Power On → Make → Grind → Pause → Brew → Ready → Pour → Stop/Overflow → Result → Replay**

The sequence is the foundation for both experience design and cinematic direction.

Preparatory actions can remain freely explorable where appropriate, but the machine's processing must obey logical prerequisites.

## **10.2 Scene 01 — Dormant Machine**

The visitor encounters the inactive machine.

The environment is calm.

The machine is visually complete and recognizable.

The cup and bean container are present as part of the initial physical composition.

The camera presents a carefully chosen three-quarter perspective.

No instructions appear.

**Emotional objective:** Curiosity.

**Visual priority:** Establish the machine's identity and reveal meaningful physical relationships.

## **10.3 Scene 02 — Hopper Discovery**

The visitor discovers and opens the hopper.

The lid responds with a believable mechanical motion.

The exposed compartment provides a physical destination for the coffee beans.

**Emotional objective:** Discovery.

**Visual priority:** Make the newly opened compartment understandable.

## **10.4 Scene 03 — Bean Preparation**

The visitor moves the coffee beans or container to the hopper and adds them.

The beans visibly fall and settle.

Sound reinforces the transfer.

The machine is now supplied with its ingredient, although it remains powered off.

**Emotional objective:** Agency and preparation.

**Visual priority:** Communicate the successful physical transfer.

## **10.5 Scene 04 — Hopper Closure**

The lid returns to its resting position.

The gesture closes the preparation interaction.

**Emotional objective:** Small completion.

**Visual priority:** Tactile closure and physical continuity.

## **10.6 Scene 05 — Cup Placement**

The visitor positions the cup below the coffee outlet.

The cup moves naturally and remains where placed.

Correct alignment should be discoverable through the visible physical relationship between the cup and outlet.

**Emotional objective:** Understanding the output path.

**Visual priority:** Establish the receiving destination before activation.

## **10.7 Scene 06 — Power On**

The visitor presses Power.

The button depresses.

The machine responds with subtle activation sounds and a restrained visual cue.

The transformation should be felt as:

**Dormant object → Active machine**

**Emotional objective:** Anticipation.

## **10.8 Scene 07 — Make Coffee**

The visitor presses Make.

The machine acknowledges the press and begins the preparation process.

The cup remains empty.

Coffee does not immediately emerge.

**Emotional objective:** Expectation.

## **10.9 Scene 08 — Grinding**

The motor begins.

Grinding becomes the dominant sound.

The machine may vibrate subtly.

Beans visually participate in the sense of processing.

Any mechanical feedback should remain grounded in plausible machine behavior.

**Emotional objective:** Tension and engagement.

## **10.10 Scene 09 — Silence / Mechanical Transition**

Grinding ends.

The scene enters a short pause.

Mechanical sound recedes.

The pause should not feel like the application has frozen.

It should feel like the machine is transitioning to the next stage.

**Emotional objective:** Suspense.

## **10.11 Scene 10 — Brewing**

The machine begins brewing.

Lower mechanical sounds, pumping, pressure, and subtle motion communicate internal activity.

The process should possess a different sound and movement character from grinding.

**Emotional objective:** Transformation.

## **10.12 Scene 11 — Coffee Ready**

The brewing process ends.

The machine settles.

An integrated physical or light-based cue may indicate readiness.

The machine does not dispense automatically.

The visitor controls what happens next.

**Emotional objective:** Anticipation followed by renewed agency.

## **10.13 Scene 12 — Pouring**

The visitor presses Pour.

Coffee begins flowing from the outlet.

The camera and lighting may emphasize the stream and cup.

The liquid moves continuously downward.

The cup begins filling if correctly positioned.

**Emotional objective:** Payoff.

## **10.14 Scene 13 — Continued Control**

The visitor can allow the coffee to continue flowing or press Pour again to stop.

The cup's position influences the visible result.

The system should respond to visitor decisions rather than simply play a predetermined successful sequence.

**Emotional objective:** Control.

## **10.15 Scene 14 — Result**

The dispensing operation concludes or is interrupted.

The machine settles into an appropriate condition.

The visible result reflects what happened during pouring.

This may include a correctly filled cup, a partial fill, a small spill, or overflow.

**Emotional objective:** Satisfaction and consequence.

## **10.16 Scene 15 — Replay**

The experience returns to a valid initial condition through the defined reset/replay mechanism.

The next visitor should be able to begin independently.

**Emotional objective:** Closure and repeatability.

# **11\. Two Modes of Experience**

## **11.1 Mode A — Exploration**

This mode emphasizes visitor agency.

The visitor can:

* Inspect the scene.  
* Orbit around the machine.  
* Discover the hopper.  
* Manipulate the beans.  
* Position the cup.  
* Investigate the physical controls.

Camera movement remains primarily user-directed.

The environment should be stable enough that the visitor can understand and manipulate the object.

## **11.2 Mode B — Machine Performance**

This mode begins when the machine enters its internal preparation sequence.

The visitor has initiated the performance, but the machine now controls its mechanical process.

Grinding, the transition pause, brewing, and readiness follow a designed rhythm.

Camera and sound may participate more actively in directing attention.

However, the visitor should not become a passive spectator for the entire experience.

The ready-to-pour moment deliberately returns control to them.

## **11.3 Core Rhythm**

The intended structure is:

**Interactive discovery → Cinematic machine performance → Interactive dispensing → Observable result**

This balance between agency and choreography defines Ritual.

# **12\. Three-Button Interaction Model**

## **12.1 Why Three Buttons?**

Three distinct controls communicate the machine's process through physical organization.

**Power** changes the machine's operating condition.

**Make** starts preparation.

**Pour** releases the result.

This is clearer than making one button change its function according to an invisible software state.

## **12.2 Power**

When the machine is inactive, Power should communicate activation through tactile and machine-integrated feedback.

Power-off and power interruption behavior must remain consistent with the conceptual machine.

During active mechanical processing, accidental input must not leave the experience in an impossible state.

The exact interruption policy is reserved for Document 03\.

## **12.3 Make**

Make controls the transition into internal preparation.

The visitor initiates the process once.

The machine then performs its mechanical sequence.

The control must not repeatedly restart or overlap preparation.

## **12.4 Pour**

Pour controls dispensing after preparation is complete.

The first press starts the liquid.

The second press stops the liquid.

The transition between flow and no flow should be visually obvious.

## **12.5 Physical Button Character**

All three controls should have believable physical feedback.

The animation should be small, intentional, and appropriate to the button's construction.

Sound should confirm actuation.

Button response must not be mistaken for confirmation that an invalid machine operation succeeded.

# **13\. Coffee Pouring & Physical Consequences**

## **13.1 Pouring as the Hero Moment**

The coffee pour is the main visual payoff.

Everything leading to it should establish anticipation.

Its visual quality must receive special attention.

The liquid should appear:

* Continuous.  
* Smooth but not unnaturally rigid.  
* Dark and warm.  
* Slightly viscous.  
* Glossy where lighting catches it.  
* Correctly positioned relative to the outlet.  
* Responsive to the dispensing control.

## **13.2 Correct Cup Placement**

When the cup is aligned beneath the outlet, coffee should enter the cup.

The internal liquid level gradually rises.

The visual response should communicate actual accumulation.

The fill should be visible from a reasonable camera angle.

## **13.3 Slight Misalignment**

When the cup is not perfectly aligned, part of the coffee may miss the opening or strike the rim.

A small spill can appear.

This interaction demonstrates that the liquid obeys the physical relationship between outlet and cup.

The exact overlap calculation and visual response belong in Document 03\.

## **13.4 Cup Moved Away**

If the visitor moves the cup away during dispensing, the liquid should not magically follow it.

Coffee should continue along the outlet's physical path, subject to the machine's dispensing state.

The surface may receive the liquid.

A small puddle can appear.

## **13.5 No Cup**

When there is no cup beneath the outlet, coffee should fall onto the receiving surface rather than fill an invisible container.

This makes the experience's cause-and-effect logic understandable.

## **13.6 Overflow**

If the visitor continues dispensing after the cup is full, the liquid may pass the rim.

The overflow should remain restrained and visually credible.

A small overflow conveys the consequence without requiring detailed fluid simulation.

## **13.7 Stop Pouring**

Pressing Pour again stops dispensing.

The liquid stream should end in a convincing way.

Any brief residual drops should remain consistent with the machine's behavior.

## **13.8 Simplified Simulation Philosophy**

The experience does not require physically accurate computational fluid dynamics.

Suitable approaches may include:

* Animated stream geometry.  
* Controlled surface movement.  
* Animated cup-fill levels.  
* Small spill meshes.  
* Limited droplets.  
* Carefully designed liquid shading.

The final rendering technique will be chosen in Documents 04 and 05\.

The underlying method may be simplified, but the perceived result should remain convincing.

# **14\. Camera Direction**

## **14.1 Camera Philosophy**

The camera should feel like part of a restrained cinematic product presentation.

It must allow meaningful inspection while preserving the quality of the composition.

The visitor should feel free to explore the object without losing the intended visual hierarchy.

## **14.2 Default Perspective**

The initial camera should use a three-quarter view.

It should reveal enough of the:

* Machine front.  
* Machine side.  
* Hopper.  
* Physical buttons.  
* Outlet.  
* Cup.  
* Supporting surface.

The composition should communicate the product's input and output immediately.

## **14.3 Controlled Horizontal Orbit**

The original target is near-complete horizontal inspection, approximately ±150–170°.

This should provide substantial freedom without exposing meaningless or problematic camera positions.

The exact range should be finalized through visual testing.

## **14.4 Restricted Vertical Movement**

Vertical camera motion should remain limited.

The visitor may inspect slightly above or below the initial viewing angle, but should not reach:

* Direct overhead views.  
* Underside views.  
* Floor-level perspectives.  
* Extreme angle distortion.  
* Camera positions that obscure the key interaction areas.

## **14.5 Controlled Distance**

Camera zoom or dolly movement should remain within a designed envelope.

Prevent:

* Entry into the geometry.  
* Extreme close-ups.  
* Excessive zoom-out.  
* Loss of object recognition.  
* Unintended camera clipping.

## **14.6 Motion Quality**

Camera control should possess damping and smooth deceleration.

The movement should feel polished and deliberate, not rigid or game-like.

There should be no uncontrolled camera spinning.

## **14.7 Cinematic Camera Participation**

During important machine events, the camera may gently direct attention.

Possible moments include:

**Power On:** A subtle adjustment emphasizing activation.

**Grinding:** A restrained move toward the active machine.

**Brewing:** A controlled change in framing that supports the mechanical process.

**Ready:** The camera settles, returning attention to the visitor's next action.

**Pour:** A subtle emphasis on the outlet and cup.

**Completion:** A composed resting frame.

The precise camera path is not yet locked.

## **14.8 Visitor Control**

Camera choreography must not compete with direct physical interaction.

The visitor's ability to inspect and operate the machine is more important than completing a decorative camera animation.

The final system must establish how manual input interacts with camera transitions.

## **14.9 Cinematic Restraint**

Avoid:

* Dramatic spins.  
* Aggressive zooms.  
* Unnecessary handheld shake.  
* Excessive camera movement.  
* Long forced cinematic sequences.  
* Rapid transitions that disorient the visitor.

The camera should guide attention, not become the subject.

# **15\. Lighting Direction**

## **15.1 Lighting as Storytelling**

Lighting is a foundational part of the experience, not a final decorative step.

It must establish physical presence, reveal material qualities, and support the most important moments.

The machine's shape and material hierarchy should remain readable throughout the sequence.

## **15.2 Key Light**

Use a large, soft primary source to define form.

The key should establish:

* Surface curvature.  
* Product silhouette.  
* Main material highlights.  
* Depth.  
* Dimensionality.

Avoid hard, artificial lighting unless a selected product reference clearly justifies it.

## **15.3 Fill Light**

Fill should preserve important detail in darker areas.

It must not eliminate shadows or make the machine appear uniformly illuminated.

The aim is controlled contrast.

## **15.4 Edge Separation**

A restrained edge or rim light can separate the machine from the backdrop.

This is especially valuable for:

* Charcoal surfaces.  
* Hopper glass.  
* Metal edges.  
* Rising steam.  
* Overall silhouette.

## **15.5 Machine-Integrated Lighting**

A physical power indicator or status light may participate in machine activation.

These lights should behave like actual appliance features.

They should not become exaggerated decorative effects.

## **15.6 Lighting Across the Sequence**

| Stage | Intended lighting behavior |
| ----- | ----- |
| Idle | Warm, calm, restrained |
| Hopper interaction | Preserve material readability and component clarity |
| Power On | Subtle integrated activation cue |
| Grinding | Maintain dimensionality and mechanical focus |
| Pause | Preserve calm and anticipation |
| Brewing | Controlled visual tension without spectacle |
| Ready | Gentle indication of completion |
| Pouring | Make dark coffee and receiving cup readable |
| Steam | Allow subtle backlight separation |
| Completion | Return to a settled, balanced composition |

## **15.7 Lighting Quality Rules**

Lighting should reveal the machine's materials rather than overwhelm them.

Avoid crushed dark details, flat frontal lighting, uncontrolled bloom, harsh reflections, or excessive color changes.

The final lighting should look intentional from every camera angle permitted by the experience.

# **16\. Motion & Animation Direction**

## **16.1 Motion Philosophy**

Motion should communicate the physical character of the object.

Different materials and mechanisms should not move identically.

A lid has weight and hinge resistance.

A button has a short mechanical travel.

A cup should follow the visitor's movement while retaining the impression of ceramic mass.

The machine's internal activity should feel mechanical.

The coffee should behave like liquid.

## **16.2 Tactile Motion**

Interaction animation should be responsive without appearing abrupt.

The first visible response should closely follow the visitor's action.

The remainder of the movement may use more deliberate easing to imply weight, resistance, and settling.

## **16.3 Mechanical Motion**

Machine movement should not resemble decorative breathing or arbitrary oscillation.

Grinding vibration, subtle internal movement, and control responses should be connected to the actual operating stage.

Motion must remain proportional to the machine's physical scale.

## **16.4 Transition Rhythm**

The sequence should alternate active mechanical moments with quieter transitions.

Grinding should feel different from brewing.

The pause should be perceptible.

Pouring should provide a contrasting fluid movement.

The scene should not maintain a constant level of animation intensity.

## **16.5 Animation Coordination**

Multiple animations may occur together during a mechanical stage.

Their relative timing must reinforce the same physical event.

For example, grinding audio, bean processing, vibration, and camera attention should feel like coordinated parts of one event.

Exact timings, easing curves, and choreography will be defined in Document 04\.

# **17\. Sound Design**

## **17.1 Sound as an Interface Language**

Since Ritual contains no conventional UI, sound plays a major role in communicating physical response and machine state.

Sound should confirm actions, suggest material qualities, and structure the passage of time.

It should feel as if it originates from the scene's physical objects.

## **17.2 Interaction Sounds**

| Interaction | Sonic character |
| ----- | ----- |
| Hopper opens | Small mechanical hinge or latch response |
| Beans added | Natural, soft rattle and settling |
| Hopper closes | Muted mechanical closure |
| Cup moved/placed | Light ceramic contact |
| Power pressed | Tactile button actuation |
| Make pressed | Distinct physical button click |
| Pour pressed | Clear but restrained control click |

## **17.3 Machine Sounds**

**Startup:** Subtle electrical/mechanical activation.

**Grinding:** The strongest and most recognizable machine sound. Substantial, textured, and mechanical.

**Mechanical pause:** Intentional contrast after grinding.

**Brewing:** Lower motor, pump, or pressure-like activity.

**Ready:** The machine settles; any readiness cue should be restrained.

**Pouring:** Soft continuous liquid sound.

**Steam:** Very subtle hiss.

**Completion:** Mechanical quiet and a return to calm.

## **17.4 Sonic Rhythm**

The conceptual sound progression is:

**Quiet → Click → Hum → Grind → Silence → Brew → Quiet → Pour → Steam → Quiet**

This rhythm gives the experience a cinematic structure without requiring a musical soundtrack.

## **17.5 Dynamic Contrast**

The grinding stage should feel more energetic than the surrounding interactions.

The pause should create contrast.

Brewing should carry a different mechanical texture.

Pouring should shift the soundscape from machinery toward flowing liquid.

The final quiet should communicate completion.

## **17.6 Sound Quality Principles**

Sound should be:

* Believable.  
* Tactile.  
* Appropriately scaled.  
* Synchronized.  
* Cohesive.  
* Restrained.  
* Physically motivated.

Avoid:

* Arcade-like effects.  
* Overly exaggerated cinematic impacts.  
* Unnecessary interface sounds.  
* Unrelated musical cues.  
* Constant mechanical noise without variation.  
* Abruptly disconnected audio transitions.

## **17.7 Sound Production Approach**

The final choice between recorded samples, procedural synthesis, or a hybrid approach should be based on sound quality and runtime practicality.

No particular audio technique is part of the creative identity.

The requirement is believable, synchronized sound.

Detailed sound assets, layering, envelopes, mixing, and implementation belong in Documents 04 and 05\.

# **18\. Emotional Pacing & Storytelling**

## **18.1 Emotional Structure**

The experience should deliberately move through a series of emotional conditions.

| Stage | Intended emotion | Visitor's internal response |
| ----- | ----- | ----- |
| Encounter | Curiosity | "What is this?" |
| Hopper discovery | Surprise | "This opens." |
| Bean preparation | Agency | "I can prepare it." |
| Cup placement | Understanding | "This is where the coffee goes." |
| Power activation | Anticipation | "The machine is alive." |
| Grinding | Engagement | "It is actually doing something." |
| Mechanical pause | Suspense | "What happens next?" |
| Brewing | Expectation | "The coffee is being prepared." |
| Ready to pour | Control | "Now I decide." |
| Pouring | Satisfaction | "The coffee is coming out." |
| Spill/overflow | Discovery | "My actions have consequences." |
| Completion | Accomplishment | "That actually worked." |

## **18.2 Waiting as an Artistic Tool**

Waiting is not wasted time.

Grinding and brewing introduce purposeful delays.

Those delays create a sense that the machine is performing genuine mechanical work.

The experience should avoid collapsing the entire process into an instantaneous transition.

## **18.3 The Importance of Silence**

The short pause between grinding and brewing should be preserved.

It creates contrast and anticipation.

Without changes in sound and motion intensity, the sequence would feel like a continuous undifferentiated animation.

## **18.4 The Coffee Pour as Payoff**

The final pour must justify the preparation sequence that precedes it.

It should be the most satisfying and visually memorable moment.

This does not require the loudest sound, strongest light, or most aggressive camera movement.

The quality should come from the accumulated anticipation and the believable response of the liquid.

## **18.5 The Visitor as Participant**

Ritual should avoid portraying the visitor as someone simply triggering an animation.

They prepare the machine, make decisions, observe the process, and control the output.

The experience should leave them feeling that they operated something meaningful.

# **19\. Discoverability & Exhibition Behavior**

## **19.1 Natural Exploration**

The visitor should be encouraged to inspect and experiment.

The initial composition should expose the most important relationships.

The interactive parts should look like real controls or movable components.

## **19.2 Interaction Clarity**

The primary interactions must be visually distinguishable from decorative geometry.

However, this distinction should emerge through physical design rather than conventional interface decoration.

## **19.3 Feedback Quality**

The response to an action should clearly indicate whether it registered.

A physical button should depress.

A lid should open.

Beans should fall.

A cup should move.

The machine should begin its corresponding process when prerequisites are satisfied.

## **19.4 Invalid Actions**

Invalid actions should not result in confusing or destructive behavior.

Where possible, the machine should communicate unavailable operations through physical logic and appropriate feedback.

Exact feedback behavior must be established in Document 03\.

## **19.5 Replay**

The experience must support multiple visitors in succession.

The machine should return to its initial condition through a reliable reset procedure.

The replay process should preserve the project's minimalist presentation and not require a conventional interface screen.

## **19.6 Desired Visitor Response**

The experience should encourage reactions such as:

* Discovering unexpectedly that the machine is interactive.  
* Wanting to operate the physical controls.  
* Watching the grinding and brewing sequence with curiosity.  
* Looking closely at the coffee stream.  
* Experimenting with cup positioning.  
* Discovering that prolonged pouring has consequences.  
* Showing another visitor how the machine works.

The exhibit should demonstrate its own creative value through participation.

The visitor interacts directly with the physical object.

**Principle 03 — The process is the narrative.**  
The story unfolds through preparation, mechanical activity, and output.

**Principle 04 — Every meaningful interaction has a physical consequence.**  
Actions should affect the machine or its environment.

**Principle 05 — The visitor controls the machine; the machine controls its internal operation.**  
This balance creates both agency and cinematic pacing.

**Principle 06 — Preparation is part of the experience.**  
The visitor supplies ingredients before activating the canonical coffee-making sequence.

**Principle 07 — Waiting creates anticipation.**  
Grinding, mechanical pauses, and brewing are essential temporal moments.

**Principle 09 — Camera movement supports attention.**

**Principle 10 — The environment frames the product.**

**Principle 11 — Physical affordances replace instructions.**

**Principle 12 — Sound makes the machine feel physical.**

**Principle 13 — Simplification is acceptable when the illusion remains convincing.**

**Principle 14 — Polish is more valuable than feature count.**

# **26\. Final Creative Statement**

Ritual transforms the familiar process of preparing espresso into a cinematic interactive experience.

The visitor encounters a believable automatic bean-to-cup coffee machine, discovers its physical components, prepares it by adding beans, positions the cup, powers the machine, and initiates the coffee-making process.

The machine grinds and brews, while deliberate sound, movement, lighting, and camera direction communicate mechanical activity and anticipation.

When preparation is complete, the machine waits.

The visitor decides when to dispense the coffee.

The liquid responds to the position of the cup, allowing correct filling, misalignment, spilling, and overflow to become visible consequences of interaction.

There is no conventional screen-based interface.

The object itself communicates its operation.

The experience remains grounded in reality, while its presentation is elevated through careful product design, material realism, motion choreography, sound, lighting, and spatial composition.

The objective is not to build the most technically complex espresso simulation.

The objective is to create a convincing, engaging, and memorable interaction in which every detail serves the same artistic direction.

**One ordinary machine.**

**One recognizable process.**

**No conventional UI.**

**The visitor prepares.**

**The machine performs.**

**The camera directs attention.**

**Sound creates physicality.**

**The coffee pour delivers the payoff.**

**The visitor completes the ritual.**
