# **1\. Document Purpose & Authority**

## **1.1 Purpose**

This document establishes how Ritual should look, move, sound, and feel.

Documents 00–03 define what the experience is, the physical assets it uses, and how its interactions behave.

Document 04 defines their cinematic and sensory execution.

It must give designers, technical artists, motion designers, sound designers, and creative developers a consistent production reference.

The objective is not simply to make each interaction visually attractive.

It is to ensure that every visual and audible element belongs to the same coherent experience.

## **1.2 Scope**

This specification covers:

* Cinematic visual identity.  
* Composition and framing.  
* Scene colors and material response.  
* Environment and atmosphere.  
* Runtime lighting.  
* Camera constraints and choreography.  
* Motion language and easing.  
* Physical interaction animation.  
* Grinding and brewing sequences.  
* Liquid and cup-filling presentation.  
* Steam, spills, and overflow.  
* Sound design and mixing.  
* Stage-by-stage cinematic timing.  
* Reset and replay presentation.  
* Adaptive rendering and reduced motion.  
* Quality standards and acceptance criteria.

## **1.3 Authority**

The artistic direction established in RITUAL-01 remains authoritative.

The behavior specified in RITUAL-03 must be preserved.

This document must not change:

* The preparation-first canonical journey.  
* Physical bean transfer.  
* Movable cup.  
* Three-button control model.  
* Grinding, pause, and brewing sequence.  
* User-controlled pouring.  
* Finite coffee volume.  
* Cup-position-dependent consequences.  
* Safe reset and replay.  
* The no-conventional-UI rule.

Presentation may reinforce these behaviors, but it must never replace or contradict them.

## **1.4 Production Decision Policy**

This document makes the remaining cinematic decisions required to establish a usable production baseline.

Values such as animation durations, light temperature, camera framing, and material parameters are intentional starting specifications.

They are subject to measured visual calibration, but their underlying design principles are settled.

A small numerical adjustment that improves the approved appearance does not constitute a new creative direction.

A major change to the scene's atmosphere, motion character, interaction visibility, or emotional pacing requires explicit design review.

# **2\. Creative Direction**

## **2.1 Central Artistic Statement**

> Make an ordinary espresso machine feel physically present, deliberately photographed, and alive through interaction.

Ritual must feel like a premium product film that the visitor can operate.

Its cinematic quality should come from restraint, controlled contrast, thoughtful timing, material realism, and meaningful consequences.

The experience should not resemble a game, a futuristic interface, or a visual-effects demonstration.

## **2.2 Visual Identity**

The approved direction is:

**Warm Minimalism \+ Premium Product Realism \+ Restrained Cinematography**

The scene should feel:

* Warm.  
* Grounded.  
* Sophisticated.  
* Tactile.  
* Quietly dramatic.  
* Physically credible.  
* Carefully composed.  
* Responsive.  
* Atmospheric without being theatrical.

## **2.3 Visual Reference Character**

The target presentation is a meeting point between:

* A premium espresso-machine product film.  
* A carefully lit design-studio photograph.  
* A believable countertop environment.  
* An interactive physical installation.

The machine is a real object.

The environment is a controlled photographic stage.

The choreography elevates the ordinary process.

## **2.4 Primary Aesthetic Decision**

The final stage will use a **warm neutral studio environment with a restrained stone-like supporting surface**.

This direction is selected over a pure black void, highly decorative interior, or brightly lit generic showroom.

The supporting surface should provide believable grounding and useful reflected light.

The background should remain dark enough to create separation, but not so black that charcoal materials disappear.

The overall image should feel warm and dimensional without being orange or sepia.

## **2.5 Key Visual Relationships**

The composition depends on four relationships:

**Dark machine / soft warm highlights**

Reveals shape and manufacturing quality.

**Transparent hopper / visible coffee beans**

Communicates preparation and ingredient presence.

**Warm ceramic / dark coffee**

Creates a readable output moment.

**Stable environment / active physical process**

Makes motion and interaction meaningful.

## **2.6 Creative Restraint**

Ritual must avoid:

* Excessive bloom.  
* Dramatic colored lighting.  
* Neon.  
* Constant particles.  
* Camera shake.  
* Aggressive dolly movements.  
* Unmotivated rotation.  
* Unrealistic reflections.  
* Exaggerated button deformation.  
* Overly synthetic steam.  
* Fantasy-like light rays.  
* Loud cinematic impacts.  
* Background music competing with machine sounds.

The experience should appear sophisticated because each detail is controlled.

# **3\. Visual Hierarchy & Composition**

## **3.1 Hierarchy**

Every frame should preserve a clear subject hierarchy.

**Primary:** Coffee machine.

**Secondary:** Hopper and bean preparation.

**Secondary:** Outlet, cup, and coffee output.

**Tertiary:** Countertop, background, and atmosphere.

Nothing else should demand comparable attention.

## **3.2 Opening Composition**

The opening shot uses a controlled three-quarter product perspective.

It should reveal:

* Front fascia.  
* One side of the machine.  
* Some upper geometry.  
* Hopper.  
* Three physical buttons.  
* Coffee outlet.  
* Cup.  
* Bean supply.  
* Supporting surface.

The visitor should understand the relationship between ingredient, machine, and cup immediately.

## **3.3 Opening Camera Side**

The baseline camera begins in front of the machine, offset modestly toward its right side.

This corresponds approximately to a front-right three-quarter view using the coordinate convention established in Document 02\.

The framing may be mirrored if the final model's actual geometry presents the controls or hopper more effectively from the other side.

The approved visual relationship matters more than the sign of the camera's X coordinate.

## **3.4 Frame Occupancy**

For the primary desktop 16:9 composition:

* The machine should occupy approximately 50–65% of the image height.  
* The principal machine-and-props arrangement should fit comfortably within approximately 70–80% of the frame width.  
* The complete machine silhouette must remain visible.  
* The beans and cup must not be cropped out.  
* The product should have enough negative space to feel carefully photographed.

These are art-direction targets rather than absolute pixel constraints.

The final camera must be calibrated against the actual geometry.

## **3.5 Composition Balance**

The machine should dominate the visual weight.

The bean container and cup should create a secondary spatial rhythm, rather than being placed symmetrically merely for decoration.

A suitable initial arrangement is:

* Bean supply toward the machine's left/front.  
* Cup toward the machine's right/front, away from correct outlet alignment.  
* Machine near the visual center.

The final positions must not interfere with physical interaction or contradict the verified asset layout.

## **3.6 Depth**

The composition should contain meaningful foreground, subject, and background separation.

Depth must be communicated primarily through:

* Perspective.  
* Object overlap.  
* Lighting.  
* Shadows.  
* Reflections.  
* Atmospheric falloff.

Do not rely on aggressive depth-of-field blur to manufacture depth.

## **3.7 Negative Space**

Empty areas are intentional.

They give the product breathing room and prevent visual fatigue.

The surrounding stage should remain quiet.

The camera should not reveal large unused spaces that make the machine appear small or insignificant.

## **3.8 Composition During Interaction**

Camera framing must remain functional.

A beautiful composition that hides the next required physical control is unacceptable.

The frame should always preserve enough context for the visitor to understand the machine.

During close-ups, avoid completely losing the relationship between the active component and the wider product.

# **4\. Color System**

## **4.1 Color Philosophy**

Ritual should use a restrained neutral palette.

The principal color relationships are:

* Charcoal.  
* Satin metallic gray.  
* Warm off-white.  
* Warm stone.  
* Natural coffee brown.  
* Subtle amber machine indicators.

No additional decorative accent color is required.

## **4.2 Baseline Color Palette**

The following values are initial sRGB art-direction references.

They do not replace physically based material calibration.

| Role | Reference color | Hex |
| ----- | ----- | ----- |
| Main body | Deep charcoal | `#252729` |
| Dark trim | Graphite | `#17191A` |
| Satin metal | Soft neutral steel | `#B7B9B7` |
| Ceramic | Warm ivory | `#F1EBDF` |
| Coffee beans | Roasted brown | `#44291B` |
| Coffee liquid | Deep espresso | `#24120B` |
| Countertop | Warm stone-gray | `#756F65` |
| Background | Warm dark gray | `#3E3C38` |
| Indicator light | Soft warm amber | `#CBA16C` |

These colors must be judged under the final runtime lighting.

A material's base color alone does not determine its rendered appearance.

## **4.3 Color Balance**

The scene should be predominantly neutral.

Warmth should emerge from:

* Light temperature.  
* Ceramic.  
* Coffee.  
* Stone.  
* Soft amber machine feedback.

Avoid strong orange color grading.

The metal should remain recognizably metallic and relatively neutral.

## **4.4 Dark Material Readability**

The charcoal machine must remain clearly separated from the background.

Its shape should be readable through:

* Broad highlights.  
* Edge separation.  
* Controlled fill.  
* Appropriate reflection sources.  
* Material roughness.

Crushing the machine into a black silhouette is not acceptable.

## **4.5 Color Consistency**

Any postprocessing or tone mapping must preserve:

* Natural ceramic whites.  
* Warm coffee browns.  
* Neutral metal response.  
* Subtle dark-surface distinctions.

Avoid heavy filters that make materials appear unrelated to their physical identities.

# **5\. Material Rendering Direction**

## **5.1 General Standard**

Every material must communicate an identifiable physical substance.

The goal is not maximum reflectivity.

It is controlled physical response.

## **5.2 Charcoal Housing**

The housing should possess:

* Semi-matte finish.  
* Broad, soft highlights.  
* Slight surface roughness.  
* Subtle curvature visibility.  
* Consistent manufactured quality.

Initial roughness target: approximately 0.45–0.65.

The surface should not look like black rubber, mirror metal, or flat unlit plastic.

## **5.3 Satin Metal**

The metal fascia and trim should use:

* Believable metallic reflection.  
* Soft, controlled highlight bands.  
* Subtle brushed direction where appropriate.  
* Moderate roughness.  
* Limited bright hotspots.

Initial roughness target: approximately 0.3–0.45.

The visible metal should appear manufactured rather than chrome-plated.

## **5.4 Hopper Glass or Transparent Plastic**

The hopper should have:

* Clear physical boundaries.  
* Controlled reflections.  
* Enough transparency to identify beans.  
* Subtle tint where appropriate.  
* Consistent visible thickness.

Avoid pure invisibility or extreme distortion.

Transparent rendering should be tuned so that the beans remain visible from the opening hero camera.

## **5.5 Coffee Beans**

Beans should vary subtly in:

* Brown tone.  
* Orientation.  
* Surface reflectivity.  
* Visible shape.

They should appear roasted and organic.

Avoid identical glossy brown shapes.

## **5.6 Ceramic Cup**

The cup should be a major contrast element.

It should have:

* Warm off-white coloring.  
* Smooth specular highlights.  
* Readable wall thickness.  
* A clear rim.  
* A visible interior.  
* Suitable shadowing.

The cup must remain readable in both empty and filled states.

## **5.7 Coffee Liquid**

The liquid should appear:

* Dark brown.  
* Glossy.  
* Smooth.  
* Slightly viscous.  
* Dimensional.  
* Responsive to scene light.

It must not resemble a uniformly colored opaque rod.

## **5.8 Supporting Surface**

The selected stage surface is a restrained warm stone/composite finish.

Target properties:

* Matte to softly satin.  
* Subtle physical texture.  
* Medium-dark warm neutral color.  
* Enough contrast with the machine and cup.  
* Acceptable reflection/shadow response.

Initial roughness target: approximately 0.75–0.9.

Avoid prominent marble veining or decorative wood grain that competes with the machine.

# **6\. Lighting Design**

## **6.1 Lighting Philosophy**

Lighting must be treated as part of the product design and cinematic narrative.

It must establish physical presence, material response, and visual focus.

The machine should be clearly readable during the entire experience.

The lighting should remain believable rather than feeling like a stage-lighting demonstration.

## **6.2 Lighting Rig**

The baseline lighting system contains:

1. Large soft key light.  
2. Restrained fill.  
3. Subtle rim/edge light.  
4. Environment reflections.  
5. Physical contact shadows.  
6. Machine-integrated indicator lighting.

Additional lights should be introduced only when they solve a specific visual problem.

## **6.3 Key Light**

The key should be a large, soft source positioned above and toward the front-left or side of the machine.

Its purpose is to:

* Reveal the body's primary curvature.  
* Create broad metal highlights.  
* Establish the machine's dimensions.  
* Illuminate the cup sufficiently.  
* Preserve soft shadow direction.

Suggested color temperature: **approximately 4000–4500 K**.

The light should be warm-neutral, not strongly orange.

## **6.4 Fill Light**

A weaker fill should preserve detail on the opposite side.

Suggested character:

* Neutral to slightly cooler than the key.  
* Soft.  
* Broad.  
* Low intensity relative to the key.

Suggested color temperature: approximately 5000–5500 K.

This slight temperature difference may create material separation without obvious colored lighting.

## **6.5 Rim Light**

A restrained rear/side light should define important edges.

It is especially useful for:

* Charcoal silhouette.  
* Hopper transparency.  
* Cup rim.  
* Metal contours.  
* Steam.

The rim should not create a glowing outline around the entire machine.

## **6.6 Environment Reflections**

Use carefully positioned soft reflection sources to reveal satin metal and hopper surfaces.

The reflection environment should resemble a plausible product-photography lighting setup.

Large reflection shapes are preferable to numerous sharp highlights.

Reflections should be stable during camera movement.

## **6.7 Contact Shadows**

The machine and props must appear physically grounded.

Contact shadows should exist under:

* Machine base.  
* Cup.  
* Bean container.

Avoid shadows that are excessively dark, too sharp, or disconnected from the objects.

## **6.8 Machine Indicator**

Use a restrained warm amber or neutral-white integrated indicator.

The indicator may change with machine operation.

It should remain a physical appliance feature.

It must not become a separate decorative light show.

## **6.9 Baseline Lighting State**

The principal studio lighting remains stable throughout the experience.

Major scene-wide color or exposure shifts are prohibited.

The machine's mechanical activity may add subtle practical-light changes, but should not repeatedly change the entire environment's mood.

## **6.10 Lighting During Pour**

The coffee stream must receive enough directional and specular lighting to remain visible.

A soft highlight should reveal liquid curvature.

The cup interior and liquid surface must remain readable.

Avoid increasing overall exposure so much that the ceramic becomes overexposed.

## **6.11 Steam Lighting**

Steam should become visible through a restrained backlight or rim relationship.

It must not require a bright glow or a large opaque white cloud.

## **6.12 Lighting Quality Gate**

Lighting is accepted only when:

* The machine is distinguishable from the background.  
* Charcoal materials retain form.  
* Metal highlights look believable.  
* Hopper contents remain visible.  
* Ceramic whites preserve detail.  
* Coffee appears dimensional.  
* The permitted camera orbit does not reveal severely broken lighting.

# **7\. Camera System & Cinematography**

## **7.1 Camera Philosophy**

The camera behaves like a restrained product cinematographer.

It should allow the visitor to inspect the machine while preserving the composition's artistic identity.

Camera movement should be:

* Smooth.  
* Controlled.  
* Deliberate.  
* Physically plausible.  
* Motivated by meaningful events.  
* Subordinate to visitor input.

## **7.2 Initial Camera**

The opening camera uses a front-right three-quarter composition.

Recommended initial settings:

| Property | Baseline |
| ----- | ----- |
| Projection | Perspective |
| Vertical field of view | Approximately 35° |
| Relative horizontal angle | Approximately 30–35° from front |
| Polar angle | Approximately 72–76° from vertical |
| Target | `MachineFocusAnchor` with composition-adjusted framing |
| Approximate distance | 1.2–1.5 m, calibrated to actual stage |
| Camera roll | 0° |

The camera must be calibrated against the verified production geometry rather than blindly using these approximate positions.

## **7.3 Orbit Model**

The visitor may orbit around the centered scene.

The experience does not use unrestricted free flight.

## **7.4 Horizontal Orbit**

Baseline horizontal range:

**−160° to \+160°**

This permits near-complete horizontal inspection while preserving a controlled experience.

The exact limits may be narrowed if the model or environment presents unsuitable views.

## **7.5 Vertical Orbit**

Baseline polar range:

**62° to 98°**

With the conventional Y-up camera model:

* Lower polar angles represent higher viewpoints.  
* Approximately 90° represents a level view.  
* Slightly above 90° permits a modest lower viewpoint.

The camera must never move beneath the supporting stage or into overhead inspection.

## **7.6 Zoom/Dolly Bounds**

The camera distance must remain constrained.

It must not:

* Enter the machine.  
* Clip through the hopper.  
* Hide the cup through excessive proximity.  
* Zoom so far away that the product loses dominance.

The range should be based on the product's bounding volume and interaction-stage extent.

An initial engineering range can be approximately 0.9–1.8 m, subject to actual asset calibration.

## **7.7 Panning**

Free camera panning is disabled.

The machine remains the center of the experience.

## **7.8 Orbit Damping**

Orbit movement should decelerate smoothly after the visitor releases input.

It must not feel rigid or slippery.

Use moderate damping.

There should be no uncontrolled camera rotation.

## **7.9 Idle Camera Behavior**

The default scene does not continuously spin the machine or camera.

The opening frame should be stable.

Extremely subtle atmospheric motion may exist, but the product composition remains calm.

This makes the visitor's first interaction more meaningful.

## **7.10 Camera Anchor System**

Cinematic camera targets should derive from the semantic anchors defined in Document 02:

* `MachineFocusAnchor`  
* `HopperFocusAnchor`  
* `PourFocusAnchor`

Camera motion should not depend on brittle guessed mesh centers.

## **7.11 Guided Camera Transitions**

Cinematic camera movement may occur during:

* Make activation.  
* Grinding.  
* Brewing.  
* Ready-to-pour.  
* Pouring.  
* Completion.  
* Reset.

The camera should not automatically perform a large move every time a minor physical object is clicked.

## **7.12 Maximum Camera Movement Character**

For ordinary state transitions:

* Favor angular adjustments within roughly 5–12°.  
* Favor restrained distance adjustments.  
* Favor smooth target changes over dramatic camera travel.  
* Avoid 180° rotations or dramatic fly-through movements.

These values describe the preferred character, not a mandate to move on every event.

## **7.13 User-Control Priority**

Manual camera movement always takes precedence.

If the visitor begins orbiting during a camera transition:

1. The active cinematic transition yields immediately.  
2. The camera continues smoothly from its current transform.  
3. The visitor gains direct control.  
4. The machine process continues unaffected.  
5. No new large camera takeover occurs until a later meaningful event.

## **7.14 Active Drag Priority**

While the visitor drags the cup or bean container, cinematic camera movement must not interfere with that gesture.

If necessary, the camera transition is cancelled or deferred.

## **7.15 Camera After Manual Exploration**

The system must never force a long automatic return from an arbitrary viewing angle merely to reach a predefined cinematic preset.

If a guided framing would require substantial unexpected rotation, it should be skipped or reduced.

This prevents the camera from fighting the visitor.

## **7.16 No Camera Shake**

Do not shake the camera during grinding or brewing.

Mechanical vibration belongs to the machine, not the viewer.

A stable camera makes subtle physical movement more convincing.

**Purpose:** Establish the machine and physical setup.

Composition:

* Front-right three-quarter view.  
* Complete machine visible.  
* Bean supply visible.

Composition:

**Purpose:** Emphasize grinding and brewing.

Composition:

* Machine remains dominant.  
* Button and hopper regions stay understandable.

Emotional character: Mechanical focus and anticipation.

**Purpose:** Preserve attention during the internal transformation.

Composition:

## **8.5 Shot E — Ready to Pour**

**Purpose:** Return agency to the visitor.

Composition:

* Pour button accessible.  
* Coffee outlet visible.

## **8.6 Shot F — Hero Pour**

**Purpose:** Emphasize the coffee stream and receiving cup.

Composition:

* Outlet readable.

## **8.7 Shot G — Completion**

**Purpose:** Present the result.

Composition:

* Cup and machine remain visually related.  
* Completed result is readable.

Emotional character: Quiet satisfaction.

## **8.8 Shot H — Reset**

**Purpose:** Restore the opening composition.

Composition: Return to Shot A.

Movement: Coordinated with the reset transition.

The camera must not expose objects teleporting or resetting unnaturally.

# **9\. Motion Design Language**

## **9.1 Fundamental Motion Character**

Ritual's motion language is:

**Controlled → Weighted → Responsive → Smooth → Restrained**

Movement should convey physical cause and material character.

## **9.2 Motion Categories**

The experience uses five main motion categories:

1. Physical button motion.  
2. Hinged mechanical motion.  
3. Direct manipulation.  
4. Motor-driven machine motion.  
5. Liquid and atmospheric motion.

Each category requires a distinct motion character.

## **9.3 Easing Principles**

**Direct manipulation:** Closely follows the pointer with minimal perceived lag.

**Button movement:** Fast actuation with a short return.

**Hopper lid:** Smooth acceleration and a weighted settling motion.

**Machine activity:** Mechanical repetition or subtle procedural vibration.

**Camera:** Smooth cinematic acceleration and deceleration.

**Liquid:** Continuous physically motivated movement.

**Steam:** Slow organic drift.

## **9.4 Easing Baseline**

| Motion category | Suggested easing character |
| ----- | ----- |
| Button press | Fast ease-out inward, controlled ease-out return |
| Hopper opening | Controlled ease-in-out with slight weighted finish |
| Hopper closing | Ease-in followed by brief mechanical settling |
| Bean container pickup | Responsive, lightly damped |
| Bean container tilt | Smooth ease-in-out |
| Bean drop | Gravity-influenced acceleration |
| Cup drag | Near-direct tracking |
| Cup release | Short ease-out settling |
| Camera | Smooth cubic or quintic ease-in-out |
| Steam | Continuous organic variation |
| Reset | Smooth subdued ease-in-out |

The exact implementation may use GSAP easing functions, spring interpolation, or equivalent motion utilities.

The perceptual result is the requirement.

## **9.5 Overshoot Policy**

Small overshoot is acceptable where physical elasticity is plausible.

Examples:

* Hopper lid settling.  
* A small ceramic placement response.  
* Selected button return motion.

Large elastic or playful bouncing is prohibited.

Ritual should not resemble a cartoon interface.

## **9.6 Timing Consistency**

Similar physical gestures should have consistent timing.

Do not make one button feel mechanical and another feel like a slow cinematic transition.

The visitor should perceive one coherent physical product.

# **10\. Interaction Motion Specifications**

## **10.1 Hopper Opening**

Target duration: **0.7 seconds**.

Motion character:

* Immediate acknowledgment.  
* Smooth hinge rotation.  
* Modest acceleration.  
* Controlled deceleration.  
* Slight weighted arrival.

No exaggerated swinging.

## **10.2 Hopper Closing**

Target duration: **0.55 seconds**.

Closing may be slightly faster than opening.

The final contact should feel precise.

A quiet latch sound supports the end position.

## **10.3 Hopper Reversal**

Document 03 permits the most recent valid target to win during repeated lid clicks.

The visual implementation must:

* Stop or retarget the existing animation.  
* Preserve the current transform.  
* Continue smoothly toward the new valid target.  
* Avoid snapping.  
* Avoid overlapping conflicting tweens.

## **10.4 Bean Pickup**

Target response latency: perceptually immediate.

A small lifted or stabilized handling pose may develop within approximately 0.15–0.25 seconds.

The container should not float exaggeratedly above the supporting surface.

## **10.5 Bean Drag**

The container closely follows the pointer.

Avoid heavy inertia that makes the object feel disconnected from the visitor.

If smoothing is used, it must remain subtle enough for precise target placement.

## **10.6 Accepted Bean Transfer**

Suggested total transfer choreography: **approximately 1.5–1.8 seconds**.

The movement contains:

1. Guided settling toward the hopper target.  
2. Controlled container tilt.  
3. Bean release.  
4. Falling and settling.  
5. Container return.

The exact subdivisions may overlap naturally rather than forming a rigid series of disconnected animations.

## **10.7 Bean Fall**

Beans should accelerate downward naturally.

Use small variation in:

* Release time.  
* Position.  
* Rotation.  
* Trajectory.  
* Settling.

Avoid uniform synchronized falling objects.

## **10.8 Bean Settling**

Beans should settle quickly without excessive bouncing.

A few subtle movements are enough to communicate physical presence.

The final hopper-filled condition must be clearly visible.

## **10.9 Invalid Bean Transfer**

When the container is released away from a valid target:

* Return to original position.  
* Use a smooth, direct motion.  
* Avoid punitive bouncing.  
* Do not simulate beans falling.

Suggested return duration: 0.35–0.55 seconds depending on travel distance.

## **10.10 Cup Drag**

The cup should track the pointer closely.

Its base remains visually connected to the stage.

Avoid excessive lifting or hovering.

## **10.11 Cup Release**

Suggested settling duration: **0.12–0.2 seconds**.

Any alignment assistance should remain subtle and confined to the small permitted snap region.

The cup should not travel several centimeters automatically after release.

## **10.12 Cup Movement During Pouring**

The cup's liquid fill surface must remain attached to the cup.

Its existing liquid volume must remain unchanged by horizontal movement.

Incoming coffee must continue to follow the machine outlet, not the cup.

## **10.13 Button Press**

The three buttons share a coherent tactile motion language.

Suggested motion:

* Inward travel: approximately 0.07–0.09 seconds.  
* Return: approximately 0.12–0.15 seconds.  
* Total: approximately 0.2–0.24 seconds.

Use the physically correct local press axis.

The exact travel distance is determined by the final button geometry, initially expected to be in the 1–3 mm range.

## **10.14 Invalid Button Press**

An unavailable button may provide a small physical depression if that is consistent with its construction.

However, the machine must not produce success-related audio, lighting, or state-driven motion.

The visitor should not be tricked into believing a rejected operation succeeded.

# **11\. Machine Animation System**

## **11.1 Mechanical Realism**

The machine should appear to perform work through restrained physical movement and sound.

There is no requirement to expose detailed internal machinery.

The desired illusion is mechanical credibility.

## **11.2 Power Activation**

Power-on should involve:

* Physical button actuation.  
* A subtle activation sound.  
* An integrated indicator becoming active.  
* A small machine response, if justified.

The body must not jump or pulse dramatically.

The transition from OFF to ON should feel precise.

## **11.3 Grinding**

Grinding is the strongest mechanical stage.

It may involve:

* Subtle chassis vibration.  
* Bean visual consumption.  
* Motor/grinder audio.  
* Very restrained mechanical detail.  
* An optional slight camera focus adjustment.

Suggested vibration amplitude:

**Approximately 0.2–0.6 mm equivalent at machine scale.**

The vibration must remain small enough that the machine does not appear unstable or weightless.

## **11.4 Grinding Motion Frequency**

A subtle high-frequency procedural vibration may be used.

The exact frequency is an implementation choice, but it should resemble a motor rather than a slow oscillating animation.

The movement should not be visibly distracting.

## **11.5 Grinding Start**

Mechanical movement should ramp in quickly rather than appearing at full intensity on the first frame.

Suggested perceptual ramp: 0.15–0.25 seconds.

## **11.6 Grinding End**

Vibration and grinder sound should decelerate or terminate coherently.

The stage must then enter the deliberate pause.

## **11.7 Transition Pause**

The pause lasts **1.2 seconds**, as established in Document 03\.

The machine should become relatively still.

Audio should recede.

This is a meaningful temporal contrast.

Do not fill the pause with unrelated decorative effects.

## **11.8 Brewing**

Brewing lasts **4.2 seconds**, as established in Document 03\.

It should feel mechanically distinct from grinding.

Possible responses include:

* Subtle low-frequency vibration.  
* Pump-like rhythmic behavior.  
* Restrained internal mechanical indication.  
* Changes in machine-integrated indicator behavior.  
* Very faint steam or warmth where appropriate.

Avoid visually identical grinding and brewing animation.

## **11.9 Brewing End**

The machine settles.

Pump sound ends.

Mechanical motion returns to rest.

A readiness cue may activate.

No liquid is dispensed.

## **11.10 Ready-to-Pour**

The machine must feel ready rather than still processing.

Visual cues should be subtle:

* Calm machine body.  
* Stable integrated indicator.  
* Lack of grinding or brewing movement.  
* No active coffee stream.

The scene should create anticipation without forcing the visitor into another automatic animation.

# **13\. Liquid Art Direction**

## **13.1 Importance**

Coffee dispensing is the primary visual payoff of Ritual.

It deserves the highest level of visual refinement among the procedural effects.

## **13.2 Core Appearance**

The liquid should look:

* Dark.  
* Warm.  
* Glossy.  
* Slightly viscous.  
* Continuous.  
* Physically plausible.  
* Appropriately scaled.

## **13.3 Stream Shape**

The stream should be relatively narrow.

A perfectly uniform cylinder is undesirable.

Subtle variations may include:

* Slight tapering.  
* Small surface waviness.  
* Minor thickness changes.  
* Natural irregularity.  
* Soft specular movement.

These variations must remain restrained.

## **13.4 Stream Radius**

A physically plausible stream diameter of a few millimeters is an appropriate starting reference.

The final radius must be calibrated to the nozzle, machine scale, and viewing distance.

The stream may be slightly art-directed for readability, but it must not resemble a thick rod.

## **13.5 Stream Continuity**

During normal pouring, the liquid should read as a continuous falling stream.

It may contain subtle surface variation, but should not break into oversized disconnected blobs.

Small droplets may appear during startup or termination.

## **13.6 Material Response**

Coffee should reflect the scene's key and edge lighting.

The material should not depend on strong emission or artificial glowing color.

Highlights must make the stream appear dimensional.

## **13.7 Liquid Color**

Use a deep warm espresso-brown base.

The stream may appear brighter near highlights or thinner regions.

The cup's liquid surface may appear darker or lighter depending on local reflections.

## **13.8 Coffee Crema**

A restrained golden-brown surface variation may be introduced at the top of the filled coffee if it improves realism.

This should remain subtle.

A thick foam layer is not required.

It must not make the cup appear to contain cappuccino or milk-based coffee.

## **13.9 Flow Startup**

When Pour begins:

1. The machine acknowledges the button press.  
2. Liquid emerges promptly from the nozzle.  
3. The visible stream extends downward.  
4. Coffee begins reaching the physical destination.  
5. Audio settles into the continuous pouring texture.

Suggested visible stream establishment: approximately 0.15–0.25 seconds.

The visual timing must remain consistent with the authoritative volume model.

## **13.10 Flow Termination**

When pouring ends:

* The stream stops at the outlet.  
* Any existing visible stream clears downward naturally.  
* A small final droplet may fall.  
* Pour sound decays.  
* Cup fill and spill remain.

The stream must not disappear instantaneously from top to bottom in a visually implausible manner.

## **13.11 Stream Destination**

The visible stream must terminate at the appropriate physical receiving surface.

If the cup is aligned, the destination is the coffee surface inside the cup.

If the cup is absent or misaligned, the destination is the stage or another appropriate physical surface.

# **14\. Cup-Filling Presentation**

## **14.1 Fill Appearance**

The liquid level inside the cup should increase visibly while coffee is captured.

The surface should remain visually connected to the cup interior.

## **14.2 Volume-Driven Fill**

The fill height must be derived from the volume model established in Document 03\.

The simulation must not independently animate a fixed fill level based on an arbitrary timer.

## **14.3 Capacity Model**

Document 03 establishes:

* Cup capacity: 1.0 normalized unit.  
* Prepared batch: 1.30 normalized units.  
* Standard pour rate: 0.125 normalized units per second.

For a fully aligned empty cup:

* Normal fill occurs in approximately 8.0 seconds.  
* Total available batch lasts approximately 10.4 seconds.

These values define the baseline visible progression.

## **14.4 Cup Surface**

The coffee surface should appear:

* Glossy.  
* Slightly curved or subtly meniscus-like.  
* Dark brown.  
* Naturally reflective.  
* Responsive to incoming liquid.

Avoid a flat, unlit brown disc.

## **14.5 Ripples**

Subtle concentric ripples or localized disturbances should occur near the incoming stream.

Ripple amplitude should remain small.

The liquid surface should not behave like an open ocean.

## **14.6 Fill Geometry**

The filling representation should match the cup's interior shape.

For a tapered vessel, fill height must be mapped to the actual interior volume.

## **14.7 Moving Filled Cup**

The liquid surface and fill body move with the cup.

Cup movement must not reset the fill level.

Because cup movement is constrained and upright, complex sloshing simulation is optional.

A tiny inertial surface response may be used, but should not imply that coffee is spilling out merely because the cup is dragged horizontally.

## **14.8 Near-Full State**

As the level approaches the rim, the surface remains visible and physically contained.

The system should not artificially stop filling merely to avoid overflow.

## **14.9 Full State**

At capacity, the liquid reaches the approved maximum normal fill level.

Further captured coffee contributes to overflow.

The fill mesh should not rise indefinitely through the cup wall.

---

# **15\. Spill & Overflow Art Direction**

## **15.1 Purpose**

Spills and overflow communicate physical consequences.

They are not failure animations or punishments.

They should feel like natural results of the visitor's actions.

## **15.2 Partial Misalignment**

When the stream only partially enters the cup:

* The visible interaction should suggest a mixed result.  
* Cup fill increases more slowly.  
* A restrained surface spill appears.  
* Liquid should not visibly pass through the ceramic rim without consequence.

A simplified visual split is acceptable if it remains coherent.

## **15.3 Complete Miss**

When the cup is outside the receiving region:

* Coffee continues falling toward the supporting surface.  
* The impact location remains beneath the outlet.  
* A small wet patch or puddle develops.

## **15.4 Puddle Appearance**

The puddle should use:

* Dark warm-brown coloration.  
* Glossy highlights.  
* Low-profile geometry.  
* Slightly irregular boundaries.  
* Gradual growth.

Avoid a perfect circular decal that looks unrelated to the flowing liquid.

## **15.5 Puddle Size**

The puddle should remain proportional to the finite batch.

Even when the full batch misses the cup, the effect should remain a small, localized spill rather than covering the product stage.

## **15.6 Puddle Growth**

Growth should be tied to actual accumulated spill volume.

The visual effect may be artistically compressed, but it must remain monotonic while spill volume increases.

It should not grow when no coffee is being spilled.

## **15.7 Overflow**

Once the cup exceeds its normal capacity:

1. The top surface reaches the rim.  
2. Additional incoming liquid crosses the rim.  
3. A restrained overflow streak appears.  
4. A small surface spill may accumulate around the cup.

## **15.8 Overflow Character**

The overflow should remain realistic in scale.

Avoid:

* Dramatic splashes.  
* Large particle explosions.  
* Excessive foam.  
* Physically impossible liquid volumes.  
* Full-screen effects.

## **15.9 Cup Movement During Pour**

When the cup moves away:

* Previously collected coffee remains.  
* New liquid falls toward the stage.  
* Spill begins or increases.

When it moves back:

* Coffee begins entering the cup again.  
* Existing spill remains visible.  
* No visual teleportation of liquid occurs.

## **15.10 Effect Persistence**

Spills and overflow remain visible after pouring ends.

They are cleared only during reset.

# **16\. Steam & Atmospheric Effects**

## **16.1 Steam Philosophy**

Steam should communicate heat and completion without becoming a decorative spectacle.

It should appear as subtle rising vapor.

## **16.2 Primary Steam Source**

The cup may emit restrained steam when hot coffee has been collected.

Steam should be visually associated with the warm liquid rather than appearing from an arbitrary point.

## **16.3 Steam Activation**

Steam may begin once meaningful hot coffee is present.

It can continue through completion and gently fade over time.

## **16.4 Empty Cup Behavior**

An empty cup should not produce coffee steam.

If all coffee misses the cup, there should be no strong steam effect rising from the empty vessel.

A faint, localized effect from freshly spilled hot coffee is optional, not required.

## **16.5 Steam Motion**

Steam should rise with:

* Gentle upward drift.  
* Small lateral variation.  
* Gradual expansion.  
* Natural dissipation.  
* Soft edges.

Avoid identical particles moving along straight vertical paths.

## **16.6 Steam Density**

Steam should remain sparse.

The machine and cup must remain visible through it.

It should never resemble smoke from a fire or a theatrical fog machine.

## **16.7 Steam Color**

Use a soft neutral to slightly warm vapor appearance.

Avoid bright pure-white particle dots.

Transparency and softness are essential.

## **16.8 Particle Budget**

Use a deliberately modest number of particles or an equivalent low-cost procedural effect.

The effect should not require a complex fluid simulation.

## **16.9 Atmospheric Dust**

Very subtle environmental dust motes are optional.

If included:

* They must be nearly imperceptible.  
* They should not look like magical sparkles.  
* They should not visibly fill the entire scene.  
* They should not distract from the machine.

The initial implementation may omit them entirely.

## **16.10 Heat Distortion**

Heat distortion is optional.

If implemented, it must be extremely restrained and localized.

Strong screen-space distortion would compromise the clean product presentation.

# **17\. Sound Design Philosophy**

## **17.1 Sound as Physical Feedback**

Ritual's lack of conventional UI makes sound especially important.

Audio should communicate:

* Contact.  
* Material.  
* Mechanical operation.  
* Process transitions.  
* Timing.  
* Anticipation.  
* Completion.

The soundscape must feel connected to the object.

## **17.2 No Background Music**

The baseline experience uses no continuous musical soundtrack.

The mechanical rhythm itself creates the sonic structure.

This keeps visitor actions clear and prevents music from competing with grinding, brewing, and pouring.

## **17.3 Sonic Identity**

The soundscape should be:

* Realistic.  
* Tactile.  
* Warm.  
* Detailed.  
* Restrained.  
* Spatially coherent.  
* Dynamically controlled.

## **17.4 Sound Priority**

Audio importance follows:

1. Primary interaction confirmation.  
2. Active machine process.  
3. Pouring.  
4. Subtle environmental detail.  
5. Optional atmosphere.

Background audio must never overwhelm the relevant action.

## **17.5 Preferred Production Method**

Use a hybrid sound-design approach.

Where high-quality recordings are available, they are preferred for recognizable mechanical actions such as grinding, bean handling, and ceramic contact.

Procedural synthesis may support subtle mechanical layers, transitions, or continuous effects.

The goal is not to use the most technically complex audio system.

The goal is convincing sound.

## **17.6 Sound Asset Requirements**

All sound assets must:

* Be properly licensed or owned.  
* Have consistent sample quality.  
* Be edited to remove unwanted background noise.  
* Have appropriate fade boundaries.  
* Avoid audible loop clicks.  
* Be normalized consistently.  
* Be suitable for browser playback.

No sound should be included solely because it is available.

# **18\. Sound Event Library**

## **18.1 Hopper Opening**

Sonic character:

* Soft mechanical hinge.  
* Small latch or movement detail.  
* Short natural decay.

Duration reference: approximately 0.25–0.6 seconds.

The sound should match the opening motion.

## **18.2 Hopper Closing**

Sonic character:

* Controlled hinge movement.  
* Subtle closure contact.  
* A small final mechanical click.

The strongest transient should align with the lid reaching its closed position.

## **18.3 Bean Container Pickup**

Sonic character:

* Very light handling sound.  
* Small contact or bean movement if appropriate.

Avoid exaggerated rattling whenever the visitor merely selects the object.

## **18.4 Bean Pour**

Sonic character:

* Natural roasted-bean rattle.  
* Multiple small impacts.  
* Irregular settling.

The sound should follow the bean release and settling rather than play as one unrelated click.

## **18.5 Cup Pickup/Movement**

Sonic character:

* Quiet ceramic contact.  
* Subtle surface movement.

A continuous dragging sound is not required.

## **18.6 Cup Placement**

Sonic character:

* Short muted ceramic clink.  
* Small contact decay.

Avoid a loud glass-like chime.

## **18.7 Physical Buttons**

All three controls should share a consistent physical button sound family.

They may vary slightly based on mechanism or context.

Button feedback should be short, precise, and tactile.

## **18.8 Machine Power-On**

Sonic character:

* Small electrical/mechanical wake-up.  
* Soft internal relay or motor response.  
* No dramatic startup impact.

Suggested duration: approximately 0.3–0.8 seconds.

## **18.9 Grinding**

Grinding should be the strongest mechanical sound in the experience.

It should contain:

* Low mechanical motor body.  
* Mid-frequency grinding texture.  
* Natural rhythmic variation.  
* Controlled high-frequency detail.

It should sound like a physical grinder rather than generic white noise.

## **18.10 Brewing**

Brewing should sound different from grinding.

Suggested elements:

* Low pump or motor activity.  
* Subtle pressure-like rhythm.  
* Small mechanical variations.  
* Controlled liquid-related detail.

It should not simply replay the grinding sound at lower volume.

## **18.11 Mechanical Pause**

The pause should be intentionally quieter.

The grinder should stop.

A small natural motor decay is acceptable, but the pause must remain perceptible.

Do not fill it with unnecessary sound effects.

## **18.12 Ready Cue**

A subtle machine-integrated cue may indicate readiness.

A soft mechanical settling sound or brief understated indicator tone is acceptable.

Avoid artificial notification chimes.

The ready state should remain understandable visually even when sound is unavailable.

## **18.13 Pouring**

Sonic character:

* Soft continuous liquid flow.  
* Small receiving-cup interaction.  
* Natural variation.  
* No excessive splashing.

The sound should begin with actual dispensing and stop when dispensing ends.

## **18.14 Spilling**

When coffee strikes the supporting surface, the sound may become slightly more splash-like.

The effect should remain small and realistic.

Do not introduce a loud failure sound.

## **18.15 Overflow**

Overflow may introduce a subtle additional trickle or wet surface sound.

It should blend with ongoing pouring rather than announcing a special game event.

## **18.16 Steam**

Use a very quiet hiss if it improves realism.

Steam audio should never dominate the scene.

## **18.17 Completion**

The soundscape should naturally return to quiet.

A restrained final mechanical settling sound may be used.

No celebratory or musical reward cue is required.

# **19\. Audio Mixing & Spatial Behavior**

## **19.1 Mixing Philosophy**

The strongest sounds should correspond to the most mechanically substantial activity.

The visitor should be able to hear important actions without being startled.

## **19.2 Relative Levels**

Use the following preliminary level hierarchy.

| Category | Relative prominence |
| ----- | ----- |
| Grinding | Highest sustained mechanical level |
| Brewing | Moderate sustained mechanical level |
| Pouring | Moderate to low, clearly audible |
| Bean transfer | Short, distinct transient |
| Buttons | Short, subtle transient |
| Hopper | Subtle mechanical sound |
| Cup | Quiet ceramic contact |
| Steam | Very low |
| Ambient room tone | Optional, extremely low |

These relationships should guide mixing rather than prescribing fixed playback volume.

## **19.3 Loudness Control**

The final mix must be evaluated on the actual exhibition speakers.

Use appropriate gain staging and a conservative master ceiling to avoid clipping.

A preliminary digital peak ceiling of approximately −3 dBFS is suitable for production testing.

Final perceived loudness should be calibrated for the venue rather than assumed from file normalization alone.

## **19.4 Dynamic Contrast**

Grinding should feel stronger than the ambient environment.

The pause should feel quieter.

Brewing should have a distinct mechanical character.

Pouring should shift the focus toward softer liquid sound.

The final completion should return to calm.

## **19.5 Sound Layering**

Layering may be used to create:

* Motor body.  
* Mechanical texture.  
* Subtle structural resonance.  
* Bean impacts.  
* Liquid flow.  
* Environmental contact.

Do not accumulate unnecessary layers that make the sound artificial or muddy.

## **19.6 Loop Design**

Grinding, brewing, and pouring sounds may require loops.

Loops must:

* Begin cleanly.  
* Sustain without obvious repetition.  
* End smoothly.  
* Avoid clicking.  
* Stop when their owning state ends.  
* Never survive reset.

## **19.7 Spatial Sound**

Subtle stereo positioning may follow the machine and physical prop locations.

Avoid extreme panning.

The experience is primarily a centered product stage rather than a large spatial audio environment.

## **19.8 Camera Independence**

Camera movement should not cause exaggerated changes in sound volume.

The machine remains physically near the visitor in the staged presentation.

Subtle spatial variation is acceptable.

## **19.9 Audio Unlock**

Browser audio may require an initial user gesture.

The first valid physical interaction should unlock audio where supported.

The experience must not require a visible "Enable Sound" overlay during normal operation.

If audio cannot start, visual interaction must remain usable.

## **19.10 Audio Reset**

Reset must:

* Stop all active loops.  
* Cancel scheduled audio events.  
* Prevent obsolete cues from playing.  
* Restore audio-system state for the next session.

# **22\. Reset & Replay Cinematography**

## **22.1 Reset Purpose**

Reset must restore the initial scene without making the experience feel like software abruptly restarting.

The behavior is defined in Document 03\.

This section defines its visual presentation.

## **22.2 Selected Reset Treatment**

Use a restrained **studio-light fade and restoration**.

Do not use a full-screen success interface, title card, or decorative transition.

The scene briefly darkens enough to conceal the discontinuous restoration of consumed beans, liquid, and object positions.

It then returns to the approved opening composition.

## **22.3 Reset Sequence**

Suggested total duration: approximately **1.3 seconds**.

### **Phase A — Settle and Dim**

Approximately 0.0–0.35 seconds.

* Active effects stop or fade.  
* Scene lighting/exposure gently reduces.  
* Current interaction is released.  
* Camera begins returning toward the hero composition.

### **Phase B — Restore**

Approximately 0.35–0.7 seconds.

* Logical state is already reset or atomically restored.  
* Hopper and beans return to initial configuration.  
* Cup becomes empty.  
* Cup returns to starting position.  
* Spill and steam clear.  
* Buttons return to rest.

The reset geometry changes should occur while the scene is visually subdued.

### **Phase C — Reveal**

Approximately 0.7–1.3 seconds.

* Normal lighting returns.  
* Hero composition is restored.  
* Scene becomes interactable.  
* Machine remains powered off.

## **22.4 Avoid Hard Visual Cuts**

Do not abruptly teleport major objects in fully visible lighting.

Do not introduce an unrelated black title screen.

The reset should feel like a deliberate cinematic restoration.

## **22.5 Reset From Any State**

The treatment must work when triggered during:

* Preparation.  
* Dragging.  
* Grinding.  
* Brewing.  
* Ready-to-pour.  
* Pouring.  
* Completion.

The camera, sound, and effects must not continue obsolete animations after reset begins.

## **22.6 Reset Input Lock**

While the restoration is active, visitor interaction remains disabled.

Input becomes available only when the reset is complete.

## **22.7 Auto-Reset Presentation**

Automatic reset uses the same visual treatment.

Do not introduce a different dramatic transition for inactivity.

# **23\. Responsive & Adaptive Presentation**

## **23.1 Primary Target**

The primary presentation target is a desktop or laptop browser in a 16:9 exhibition display environment.

## **23.2 Responsive Composition**

The experience should adapt its camera framing rather than arbitrarily scaling the physical machine.

The object remains physically consistent.

The camera may adjust distance or framing to keep the relevant setup visible.

## **23.3 Narrower Viewports**

On narrower screens:

* Preserve the machine's recognizability.  
* Avoid cropping interactive props.  
* Adjust camera distance.  
* Maintain a useful view of controls and outlet.  
* Keep pointer or touch interactions reachable.

The scene should not add conventional navigation or instructional UI merely because the viewport is smaller.

## **23.4 Quality Adaptation**

Visual quality may adapt to device capability.

Possible lower-cost alternatives include:

* Reduced shadow resolution.  
* Reduced reflection quality.  
* Fewer steam particles.  
* Simpler postprocessing.  
* Reduced environmental effects.  
* Lower dynamic pixel ratio.

## **23.5 Non-Negotiable Quality**

Adaptive quality must not remove or break:

* Primary machine geometry.  
* Hopper interaction.  
* Bean transfer.  
* Cup movement.  
* Machine-state behavior.  
* Coffee stream.  
* Cup filling.  
* Spill consequences.  
* Reset.

Core interaction correctness always takes priority over optional visual effects.

## **23.6 Reduced Motion**

When reduced motion is requested by the environment or browser preferences:

* Minimize nonessential camera transitions.  
* Disable decorative camera drift.  
* Reduce excessive atmospheric motion.  
* Preserve essential physical feedback.  
* Keep machine-state timing coherent.  
* Avoid abrupt or flashing effects.

The visitor must still understand the physical process.

## **23.7 Audio-Off Operation**

All primary interactions must remain usable without sound.

Machine movement and integrated visual feedback must communicate essential state changes.

Audio enhances the experience; it is not the sole source of critical information.

# **24\. Performance-Aware Art Direction**

## **24.1 Principle**

Visual quality should be evaluated against computational cost.

A subtle effect that consumes substantial GPU resources without improving the experience should be simplified or removed.

## **24.2 Performance Priority**

Prioritize rendering resources in this order:

1. Machine geometry and materials.  
2. Reliable interaction.  
3. Correct camera and lighting.  
4. Coffee stream and cup fill.  
5. Main mechanical animation.  
6. Necessary shadows and reflections.  
7. Steam.  
8. Secondary atmosphere.  
9. Decorative postprocessing.

## **24.3 Frame-Rate Target**

Preferred presentation target:

**Stable 60 FPS on the approved exhibition hardware where feasible.**

The final acceptance target must be confirmed during device profiling.

## **24.4 Frame Independence**

Animations must use time-based progression.

Liquid volume must use the authoritative simulation time established in Document 03\.

Particles and secondary motion must not accelerate or slow down merely because the frame rate changes.

## **24.5 Postprocessing Policy**

Postprocessing should remain restrained.

Potential effects:

* Filmic tone mapping.  
* Selective subtle bloom.  
* Minimal vignette.  
* Very restrained depth of field.

Avoid stacking expensive passes without a measurable visual benefit.

## **24.6 Bloom**

Bloom is not a default decorative requirement.

If used, it should affect only genuinely bright highlights or physical indicators.

The charcoal body must not glow.

## **24.7 Depth of Field**

The default experience should preserve enough sharpness for interactive objects.

Strong depth of field may hide the hopper, controls, or cup.

The baseline should favor broad product readability.

## **24.8 Motion Blur**

Heavy motion blur is not required.

It may introduce visual artifacts and complicate interaction clarity.

Do not use it without a specific demonstrated benefit.

## **24.9 Steam Optimization**

Use inexpensive procedural particles or lightweight shader effects.

Avoid full volumetric fluid simulation.

## **24.10 Liquid Optimization**

Use a controlled procedural stream and fill representation rather than a full fluid solver.

Prioritize visual appearance and spatial coherence.

## **24.11 Asset Reuse**

Reuse suitable geometry, materials, textures, and buffers.

Avoid continuous creation and disposal of new GPU resources during ordinary animation frames.

## **24.12 Quality Tiers**

The implementation may provide internal high/medium/low visual quality tiers.

These must not introduce visible UI.

Tier selection may be automatic or configured by the exhibition operator.

The machine behavior remains identical across tiers.

# **25\. Visual and Audio Synchronization Contract**

## **25.1 Single Source of Truth**

Machine events defined in Document 03 must drive presentation.

Camera, sound, light, and animation must not independently invent machine stages.

## **25.2 Event Synchronization**

For each significant event, the runtime should coordinate:

* State.  
* Motion.  
* Audio.  
* Lighting.  
* Camera.  
* Effects.

The presentation may delay or layer visual responses for cinematic purposes, but the system's logical behavior remains authoritative.

## **25.3 Important Synchronization Events**

| Event | Required coordination |
| ----- | ----- |
| Hopper opened | Hinge animation and mechanical audio |
| Beans transferred | Container tilt, falling beans, rattling, hopper-fill update |
| Power on | Button motion, startup audio, indicator activation |
| Make accepted | Button feedback, process timeline start |
| Grinding start | Motor audio, subtle vibration, bean processing |
| Grinding end | Motor decay, vibration settling |
| Brewing start | Pump audio and distinct mechanical motion |
| Brewing end | Sound stop, machine settling, readiness feedback |
| Pour start | Button press, stream, liquid sound |
| Cup capture | Fill geometry and liquid-surface response |
| Surface miss | Impact visual and spill accumulation |
| Overflow | Rim overflow and surface response |
| Pour end | Stream termination, audio decay, completion |
| Reset | Cancel all active effects and restore initial presentation |

## **25.4 Timing Consistency**

Audio and visible physical responses should align closely enough that visitors perceive one event.

A button click should not occur noticeably after the button has already returned to rest.

A motor should not keep sounding after its related animation has ended.

A cup should not fill before the coffee visibly reaches it.

## **25.5 Reset Cancellation**

All active sensory sequences must be cancellable.

A reset must prevent old camera tweens, scheduled sounds, or shader-driven effects from reappearing in the new session.

Its machine should look manufactured, weighted, and real.

Its camera should behave like a careful observer.

Its completion should return the scene to calm.

The experience should not rely on visual excess, aggressive animation, complicated simulation, or instructional interface elements.

Every movement should have a reason.

Every sound should belong to a physical event.

Every lighting decision should reveal form or reinforce atmosphere.

Every camera transition should improve attention without removing control.

Every effect should contribute to the same artistic identity.

**A deliberate process.**

**A restrained cinematic language.**
